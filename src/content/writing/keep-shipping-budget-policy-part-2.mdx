[Part 1](/writing/keep-shipping-copilot-budget-limits-part-1/) ended with a
sketch. When someone's AI budget runs out mid-task, their budget owner can
approve a small grace allowance that expires and only buys a cheaper model.
The sketch reads well, and that's its weakness. It never has to say which
limit stopped the person, what the automation may touch at each step, or what
happens when a step only half works.

This part writes those decisions down as policy. It's still a design for a
private prototype. Nothing here has run in a pilot, and I have no savings to
report. What I can show is where writing it down changed the design.

## Which budget applies

A blocked request doesn't say which limit blocked it. Limits nest: a person's
cap sits inside a team limit, which sits inside an organization limit, and a
request goes through only if every one of them still has room.

The grace path raises exactly one of those, the person's own cap. That makes
the first rule about eligibility, not money:

> Grace applies only when the person's own cap is the limit that stopped them,
> and they haven't had grace this cycle.

If a team or organization limit is the one that's exhausted, raising the
person's cap changes nothing. The request is still blocked one level up, and
the decision belongs to whoever owns that limit. That's a budget conversation,
not a way to finish one task.

Part 1 left open whether grace should be sized in money, tokens, or tasks.
Writing the rule settled it for me: in the cap's own unit. The cap is what the
platform enforces, so it's the only unit where "approve 20 more" means the same
thing to the approver, the automation, and the enforcement point. A token count
costs different amounts on different models, and nothing meters "one task".

## Three states and a forbidden corner

As steps, the grace path is a line: stop, approve, remove, verify, raise. As
states, it's a small machine, and each state is defined by two facts: which
models the person can reach, and what their cap is.

- **Normal, then stopped.** Every approved model, normal cap. When the cap
  runs out, the platform blocks requests. The automation does nothing here.
- **Restricted.** The owner approved grace, so the broad grant is gone. The cap
  hasn't moved, so the person is still stopped. They stay here until the owner
  sees the restriction in the clients the person actually uses.
- **Grace.** Cheaper model only, cap raised once by the approved amount. When
  that's spent, the person is stopped again until the cycle resets.

The fourth combination is the one that must never happen: a raised cap while
the broad grant is still in place. That's grace paying for every model, the
outcome the whole design exists to prevent.

Laying the states out by those two facts showed me something the step list
hid. Part 1's rule, restrict which models first and then raise how much, has a
mirror image on the way back. At the end of the cycle, the reset has to lower
the cap first and only then return the broad grant. In the other order, the
person passes through the forbidden corner. Usually that lasts a moment and
nobody notices. If the reset job fails between its two steps, it lasts until
someone does.

Railway signal boxes solved the same problem with interlocking: a lever can't
move until the levers it depends on are in place, so a dangerous combination
can't be set, however the signaller hurries. The grace path needs that
property more than it needs speed. Every step moves toward the safe side, so a
job that dies halfway leaves the person stopped or restricted, never in the
forbidden corner.

## What each transition may change

This table is the policy. Each transition has one trigger, one actor, and one
thing it may change. Anything outside the last column is out of bounds for that
step, even if the automation has permission to change it.

| Transition | Trigger | Who acts | Changes only |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Normal → stopped | The person's cap runs out | The platform | Nothing; it blocks requests |
| Stopped → normal | The owner approves a full increase | Owner approves, job applies | The cap |
| Stopped → restricted | The owner approves grace | Owner approves, job applies | The person's model-access groups |
| Restricted → grace | The owner sees the restriction where the person works | Owner approves, job applies | The cap, once, by the approved amount |
| Grace → stopped | The grace is spent | The platform | Nothing; it blocks requests |
| Any state → normal | The cycle resets | The job, on a schedule | The cap, then the model-access groups |

The "who acts" column keeps the automation from approving its own spending.
Every change to money or access starts with a person, and the platform enforces
the stops. The automation only carries out what someone already approved.

Writing the last column exposed the increments-versus-totals trap from
Part 1 again. "Raise the cap by 20" applied twice is a different policy from
"raise the cap by 20" applied once. So a transition here records totals, not
increments: the cap it expects to find and the cap it sets. A retry that finds
the cap already at the target does nothing. One that finds any other value
stops and asks, because someone else changed the cap in between.

## What must stay untouched

Some things sit outside every transition. Writing them as a list made them
testable: each one is a check that should fail loudly, not a convention
someone has to remember.

- **Higher limits.** No transition changes a team or organization limit. If one
  of those is exhausted, grace doesn't apply.
- **Other permissions.** Model access lives in dedicated groups. No transition
  touches a group that also controls repositories, licenses, or anything else.
- **Tracked consumption.** Moving someone between groups doesn't reset what
  they've used, or make them eligible for grace again.
- **The approval.** The automation never approves its own increase, and one
  approval covers one transition, for one person, in one cycle.
- **Open sessions.** The policy can't change what a client has cached, so it
  doesn't pretend to. That's why verification is a person looking, not a
  timer.

## What writing it down didn't settle

- **Verification is still manual.** Without a supported signal that every
  client has applied the restriction, the restricted state ends when a person
  says so. I'd rather have a slow, honest gate than a fast guess, but it's the
  step most likely to make the pause too long to be useful.
- **"Once per cycle" is a choice, not a law.** Someone who hits the cap early
  in the cycle and again near the end may need a second grace more than
  someone who hits it on the last day. I haven't found a rule for that which
  stays simple to audit.
- **The cheaper model might still not finish the task.** The policy makes the
  grace path safe to run. It says nothing about whether it's worth running.
  That needs the pilot Part 1 described: task completion, total cost including
  retries, time spent blocked, and the owner's effort.

Writing a grace path as states and invariants doesn't make it cheaper or
faster. It makes it checkable, and that's what a budget owner needs before
approving anything.
