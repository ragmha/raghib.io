In a few weeks, three AI labs shipped models that can't write a sentence.

- **OpenAI** opened a
  [Decisions API](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/decisions) in
  public beta. It returns a probability, a choice or a score, and nothing else.
- **TypeSafe**, a new lab founded by an early ChatGPT researcher, launched
  [Jev](https://typesafe.ai/blog/introducing-system-one-models-and-jev), a
  "System One" model whose outputs are typed values defined in advance.
- **Microsoft** released
  [Decision-1](https://commandline.microsoft.com/microsoft-decision-1-model-foundry/)
  in Foundry and on OpenRouter, built for routing, classification and
  verification.

One launch is a feature. Three is a category. Decision models are no longer a
side option on a chat API. They're becoming a layer in their own right: the
smart if-statement inside software.

Here's why that matters. Look closely at a real AI workflow and most of the
calls aren't the hard problem. They're small decisions around it. Which team
owns this ticket? Is this tool call safe? Is this answer good enough to ship?
Does this task need the expensive model?

We've been asking frontier LLMs to make those calls because they could. That
works, but it's slow, expensive and hard to audit. My view is simple:
**spend the large models on thinking, not on routine choices.**

Before anything gets built, I use the most capable model I have to question
the plan. It asks one question at a time, works through every branch, and
doesn't stop until the scope is clear. (I use Matt Pocock's
[grill-me](https://github.com/mattpocock/skills/tree/main/skills/productivity/grill-me)
skill for this.) That's the expensive step, and it's
worth it, because a vague plan wastes far more than tokens. Once the plan is
ready, the many small calls along the way can go to something small,
calibrated and logged.

## Two releases, two layers

**Microsoft-Decision-1**, in Microsoft Foundry and on OpenRouter, is a
*decision model*. You give it a fixed set of options, such as yes/no, a list of
labels or a rubric score. It returns a calibrated probability for each option
in a single pass. It doesn't write prose. Microsoft
[reports](https://commandline.microsoft.com/microsoft-decision-1-model-foundry/)
the top accuracy across a 36-benchmark comparison. It also reports P50 latency
about 35× lower than GPT-6 Sol and a 1.3% average decision-flip rate under
input perturbations. Input costs $0.042 per million tokens, and output is free.

**HydraFusion**, a research preview in GitHub Copilot, is *multi-model
orchestration* for coding. For each task it chooses one of three workflows:

- **Single:** one model does the task.
- **Cascade:** a cheaper model drafts, then a quality gate either accepts the
  result or escalates to a stronger model.
- **Critique:** a read-only critic from a different model family reviews the
  draft, then the drafter revises once.

In GitHub's
[offline evaluation](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/github-copilot/project-hydrafusion-frontier-quality-via-multi-model-orchestration/),
HydraFusion scored 4.9 points higher than Claude Opus 5 on TerminalBench 2.1 at
67% lower estimated cost. It finished within 1.5 points on DeepSWE at 36% lower
cost.

They don't compete. They answer the same question at different layers:
*which effort should this task get?*

<DecisionAnatomy variant="layers" />

| Dimension | Decision model (Decision-1) | Orchestrator (HydraFusion) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| What it is | A building block: score fixed options | A finished workflow: route, draft, critique, escalate |
| Where it runs | Your apps, agents, pipelines | Copilot CLI, VS Code, Copilot app |
| Output | Probabilities you can threshold and log | One coherent change set |
| You control | Options, thresholds, fallbacks | Model choice, admin enablement, review |
| Best for | High-volume, structured, auditable calls | Hard, open-ended coding tasks |
| Maturity | Generally available model | Research preview |

Here's where I land. **Orchestration belongs inside the tools; decisions belong
inside your systems.** Developers shouldn't have to pick models by hand any
more than they pick CPU cores. But the decisions that encode *your* policy, such
as who owns what, what is safe and what is good enough, are yours to define,
test and own, whichever vendor's model makes them.

Look at the Cascade pattern. Its quality gate is exactly the kind of call a
decision model is designed for. I expect most internal agents will end up
needing the same three parts: a router, a gate and a judge.

## Why I think this matters at scale

Decision calls add up. The announcement shows how quickly latency compounds:
20 sequential decisions at an extra 100 ms each add two seconds to a workflow.
Cost compounds too. At the published price, one million decisions with
500-token inputs cost about **$21** in input tokens. Calls like this used to
need a frontier model.

Probabilities matter even more, and I think this is the underrated part. A
label tells you nothing you can govern. A
calibrated 0.93 lets you set a policy: act automatically above 0.9, send
anything between 0.6 and 0.9 to a person, reject the rest. You can audit that
policy, tune it and explain it to anyone who asks why.

## Eight experiments I'd run first

I don't trust any vendor's benchmark more than a team's own data, mine
included. Each experiment below is small enough for a two-week pilot, uses data
you already have and ends with a number. Swap in whichever models you already
use.

<DecisionAnatomy variant="experiments" />

### Delivery

**1. Build your own benchmark for HydraFusion.** Take 30 to 50 recently closed,
well-scoped issues that have tests. Replay each one from its parent commit with
a fixed frontier model, then with HydraFusion. Compare tests passing, review
comments, wall-clock time and premium-request usage. GitHub built CheckpointBench
from real sessions; this is the same idea with your codebase.

**2. Route issues before anyone reads them.** In a GitHub Actions workflow on
`issues: opened`, ask Decision-1 to choose type, severity and owning team. Use
CODEOWNERS teams as the option list. Compare its labels with 500 historical
human labels, then apply labels only above a confidence threshold. Report
accuracy, coverage at that threshold and median time-to-first-response.

### Security

**3. Gate agent tool calls.** Put a decision step in front of every
agent tool call, including MCP calls. The options are allow, block and
escalate. Run a red-team set of prompt-injection and data-exfiltration attempts
alongside ordinary traffic. Report the catch rate *and* the false-block rate.
Blocking safe calls is how security controls get switched off.

**4. Triage findings, don't replace scanners.** Score existing code scanning
and dependency alerts for likely exploitability using repository context. Check
the ranking against alerts your security team has already triaged. The scanner
still finds issues; the decision model decides what humans look at first.

### Scalability

**5. Measure the cost-latency curve.** Classify 100,000 real records, such as
support tickets, incident log lines or survey responses. Use Decision-1 and the
LLM you use today on the same labels. Plot p50 and p95 latency, throughput and
cost per thousand decisions.

**6. Find the sequential-decision ceiling.** Take an agent loop with many
decision points: should it continue, retry, stop or hand off? Replace only those
decision points with a decision model and keep generation unchanged. Measure
end-to-end task time and success rate. If success holds while time drops, you
have found the waste.

### Governance

**7. Prove the confidence is calibrated.** On 1,000 labelled examples, bucket
predictions by confidence. A decision made at 90% confidence should be right
about nine times in ten. Publish the reliability diagram with the act, review
and reject thresholds. Log every decision with its probability, options and
model version.

**8. Test consistency.** Write ten policy decisions as rubrics, for example
"does this pull request change require a security review?" Paraphrase each
input, reorder the options and add formatting noise. Count how often the
decision changes. Same facts, same decision is the oldest governance
requirement; now you can measure it. Then make the decision a required status
check through a branch ruleset so the policy runs on every pull request.

## How I'd run the pilot

I wouldn't start with a model comparison. I'd start with one decision that
happens often, has a clear answer and costs something when it goes wrong.
Then I'd work through six steps:

<DecisionAnatomy variant="pilot" />

1. **Pick one decision.** Issue routing, for example, not the whole support
   workflow. Define the options, who owns the decision and what a mistake costs.
2. **Measure today's baseline.** Record accuracy, time to resolution and cost
   per successful outcome, including retries and human corrections. A cheaper
   model call isn't necessarily a cheaper workflow.
3. **Replay historical data.** Use labelled examples the model hasn't been
   tuned on, including ambiguous and uncommon cases. Compare against the current
   approach, whether that's a person, a rule or another model.
4. **Set act, review and reject thresholds.** Choose them from measured
   calibration and the consequences of each error, not a convenient number.
   Define what happens when the model times out or can't make a reliable choice.
5. **Run in shadow mode.** Log predictions without changing real outcomes.
   Compare them with actual decisions, capture disagreements and check whether
   the proposed review queue would reduce work or create more interruptions.
6. **Expand only when the evidence holds.** Agree on acceptable quality,
   latency and total cost before the pilot. Roll out gradually only if it meets
   those targets, with an owner, versioned decision logs and a rollback path.

For me, that's the bridge between a convincing demo and a useful system:
**a cheap decision is only useful if it improves the outcome of the whole
workflow.**

## Where I could be wrong

- **Small models may not stay cheaper.** If frontier models keep getting
  faster and cheaper, a separate decision layer may become one more thing to
  maintain. I still think the auditability argument survives that.
- **Fixed options can hide the real answer.** Some decisions don't fit a
  list, and forcing them into one could create false confidence.
- **Reported is not proven.** Both sets of numbers come from the vendors, on
  their benchmarks. HydraFusion's results are offline and specific to the
  evaluated benchmark revisions, model pool and pricing assumptions.
- **A preview is not a production commitment.** HydraFusion's names,
  workflows and availability may change.
- **Know where your data goes.** If inputs are sensitive, run the model
  somewhere your existing data controls already apply.

## The takeaway

I believe the next efficiency gain in AI won't come only from smarter models.
It will come from putting them where they matter. Use the strongest model to
plan and scope until the work is ready. Make cheap, calibrated, logged
decisions everywhere after that. Use expensive generation only where it earns
its cost.
Then prove both with your own data, not someone else's benchmark.
